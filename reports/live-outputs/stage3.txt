The foundational case law concerning consequential damages for breach of contract is the English decision ***Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854)**. This case established the "rule of remoteness," which remains the standard in common law jurisdictions worldwide.

### The Rule of *Hadley v. Baxendale*
The court held that a non-breaching party may recover damages for a breach of contract only if the losses were:
1.  **Naturally arising:** Damages that arise naturally, according to the usual course of things, from the breach itself.
2.  **In the contemplation of the parties:** Damages that may reasonably be supposed to have been in the contemplation of both parties at the time they made the contract as the probable result of a breach.

### Legal Significance
*   **Foreseeability:** The ruling limits liability by requiring that the damages be foreseeable at the time of contracting. If a loss is too remote or unexpected, the breaching party is not liable for it.
*   **Communication of Special Circumstances:** The case established that if a party has special circumstances that could lead to unusual losses in the event of a breach, they must communicate these risks to the other party at the time of contracting. Failure to do so generally precludes the recovery of those specific consequential damages.

### Modern Application
While *Hadley v. Baxendale* originated in the 19th century, it is consistently cited in modern contract law. Courts today use this framework to distinguish between:
*   **Direct (General) Damages:** Losses that flow naturally from the breach (e.g., the difference in value between the goods promised and the goods delivered).
*   **Consequential (Special) Damages:** Losses that result from the specific circumstances of the non-breaching party (e.g., lost profits from a third-party contract that could not be fulfilled due to the breach).

Because consequential damages can be substantial, many commercial contracts include "limitation of liability" clauses that explicitly waive or cap the recovery of consequential, incidental, or indirect damages to mitigate the risks defined by this precedent.
